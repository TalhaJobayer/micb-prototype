Choosing your first telescope

Start with how you plan to use it: a stable mount and easy setup usually matter more than the biggest number on the box. Larger apertures gather more light, but a telescope you can carry and set up quickly gets used more often. Ask the store to confirm model details before buying.

Telescope magnification explained

Magnification equals the telescope’s focal length divided by the eyepiece’s focal length. A 2× Barlow lens doubles the effective focal length, so it doubles the magnification of any eyepiece. Higher magnification is not always better: it dims and blurs the image, and the atmosphere limits useful power.

Beginner’s guide to microscopes

Begin with the lowest magnification to find and centre your specimen, then move to higher objectives. Thin, translucent samples work best, and good lighting makes a big difference. Digital microscopes show the sample on a screen, which suits group learning.

Exploring the night sky

The Moon is the easiest first target and shows craters even in small instruments. Give your eyes about 20 minutes to adapt to the dark, avoid bright screens, and check the weather and Moon phase before you head out.

Refractor vs reflector telescopes

Refractors use lenses and are sealed and low-maintenance; reflectors use mirrors and typically offer more aperture for the price, but may need occasional alignment (collimation).

Beginner guide to astronomy binoculars

Binoculars give a wide field of view that suits star fields and the Moon. A figure like 10×50 means 10× magnification with 50 mm objective lenses; steady holding or a tripod helps a lot.
